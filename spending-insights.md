# Spending Insights

The **Spending Insights** page supports reviewing spending patterns and identifying areas for further investigation.

Use it to move beyond a single total and consider what is driving spending, whether a change is persistent, and how much it matters to the overall result.

## Start with scope and period

Check the selected dates and accounts before interpreting a pattern.

A partial current month can appear lower than a completed month simply because fewer days have been recorded. Excluding an account can also change the apparent category mix.

Use equivalent periods and consistent selections for comparisons.

## Dollar impact and relative size

Read amounts and proportions together.

A category can have a large percentage increase while contributing little to the overall change. A major expense category can have a smaller percentage increase but a much larger dollar effect.

For example:

| Category | Earlier period | Later period | Increase |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Category A | $50 | $100 | $50, or 100% |
| Category B | $2,000 | $2,200 | $200, or 10% |

Category A grew faster in percentage terms. Category B contributed four times as much additional spending.

## Recurring patterns and exceptional items

Separate normal recurring spending from occasional events.

Annual renewals, travel, repairs, and other irregular items can produce a spike. A spike deserves explanation, but one period alone does not establish an ongoing trend.

Repeated increases across several comparable periods provide different evidence from a single unusually high month.

## Category mix

A category's share can change because that category changed, because other categories changed, or because filters changed the total used for comparison.

If a share looks surprising, check the underlying dollar amount on **Income Expense** and the comparison base on **Income Expense Percent**.

A lower share does not necessarily mean a lower cost.

## Investigate before interpreting

Use **Category Detail** to examine the activity behind an unusual result.

Check for refunds, reversals, duplicate-looking entries, classification changes, and differences in period coverage. An unusual result is a reason to investigate; it is not by itself proof of an error or a lasting change.

When reviewing a displayed indicator, read its label together with the supporting amounts and selected period.

## From observation to planning

After identifying a pattern, compare it with the budget and the expected future cash position.

A recurring expense increase may justify reviewing the plan. A one-off item may instead need a separate explanation so it is not treated as a normal monthly cost.

Use **Actual vs Budget** for the planning comparison and **Cash Flow** for cash-availability implications.

## Practical uses

- Identify categories driving changes in spending.
- Compare dollar impact with percentage growth.
- Separate repeated patterns from occasional expenses.
- Investigate unusual results before drawing conclusions.
- Support budget reviews with transaction and period context.
